Knowledge Base

/knowledge-base Public

The platform's searchable, image-rich library of materials and knowledge documents — built automatically from supplier PDFs and catalogues. Because it's public, it doubles as a shareable catalogue you can send to a customer or open without signing in. This guide teaches what the library is, how its multi-signal search actually works, how to search and filter, how to act on a result, and why it's public.

How to open it: open the public Knowledge Base at /knowledge-base (no sign-in required).

1. What the Knowledge Base is

What it is — the front door to everything the platform knows about materials. When a supplier PDF or catalogue is processed, the platform's extraction pipeline reads it page by page, finds each product, lifts out its imagery, recognises its specifications, and writes a clean, structured entry.

Why it matters — the result is a single library where every material is consistent and searchable — no matter which supplier or catalogue layout it came from. Each entry typically carries product imagery, a description, technical specifications, and a link back to the catalogue it came from.

What it affects elsewhere — because the page is public, it works equally well as your internal reference library and as a customer-facing catalogue you can share. Everything you find here can be carried into moodboards, quotes, comparisons, and the Agent Hub.

2. How multi-signal search works

What it is — search here is not a plain keyword match. The platform compares your query against several different signals at once and blends them into a single relevance score, so you can find materials by what they are and by what they look like — described in your own words.

Why it matters — because the signals are combined, natural-language queries work well. Searching for "warm matte stone for a bathroom floor" weighs the words, the look, the colour family and the surface together — rather than only matching the exact characters you typed.

The signals that get blended

SignalWhat it captures
TextThe words on the entry — name, description and written specifications.
Visual appearanceHow the material actually looks in its images, so a photo or a "looks like…" phrase can match.
UnderstandingAn AI reading of each material — what it is, where it's used and how it would be described.
ColourThe dominant colours and tones of the material.
TextureSurface character — smooth, grainy, ribbed, matte, glossy, and so on.
StyleThe overall aesthetic — modern, rustic, industrial, classic.
MaterialThe underlying material family — ceramic, wood, stone, metal, textile.

What it affects elsewhere — the same blended engine powers conversational and image search in the Agent Hub and material identification in Recognition — so the way you phrase a query here transfers directly to those surfaces.

Tip: describe the feel you want, not just the name. "Soft grey concrete-look with a fine texture" usually returns more useful results than a single keyword, because the visual and texture signals have something to work with.

3. Searching and filtering with facets

What it is — alongside the search box, facet filters let you narrow the library by its structured attributes. Pick one or more values and the results update to match.

Why it matters — facets give you precise, structured control to complement the fuzzy, natural-language search — letting you combine "describe the feel" with hard constraints like a specific material family or finish.

How to search and filter

  1. Type a query in the search box — a name, or a natural-language phrase describing what you want. Results filter as you type.
  2. Open the facet filters beside the search box and pick one or more values to narrow the library:
    • Category — the type of product (for example tiles, flooring, fixtures).
    • Colour — filter to a colour family.
    • Material — the material family the item is made from.
    • Finish — surface finish such as matte, polished or textured.
    • …and more — additional facets appear automatically based on what the catalogues contain.
  3. Combine facets with a text or natural-language query to zero in quickly.

Facets are populated from the structured data the pipeline extracts, and equivalent values across languages and spellings are grouped together — so "white", for instance, gathers its variants under one filter instead of scattering across dozens of near-duplicates.

What it affects elsewhere — the facets reflect the structured attributes extracted from your processed catalogues; the cleaner and more complete those catalogues are, the richer the filters become.

4. Opening a material

What it is — the detail view you reach by selecting any result.

Why it matters — it's where you confirm an item is what you need and trace any figure back to its source before you put it in front of a client.

What you'll find inside

  • Imagery — the product photos and crops the pipeline extracted from the catalogue.
  • Specifications — the recognised technical attributes (dimensions, finish, material and other catalogue details).
  • Source — a reference back to the catalogue or document the entry was built from, so you can trace any figure to its origin.

What it affects elsewhere — from the detail view you can carry the material into the rest of the platform — see below.

6. Conversational and visual search

What it is — for anything that's easier to describe than to filter — "something like travertine but darker", or finding matches for a photo you upload — the Agent Hub runs the same blended search engine.

Why it matters — the difference is that it works as a conversation: you can refine across several turns, attach images, and it will explain why each result matched (the colour, the style, the material it picked up on).

How to use it

  1. Open the Agent Hub and describe what you want, or attach an image.
  2. Read the explanation of why each result matched, then refine your next message to steer the results.
  3. If you only want to confirm whether an item in a photo exists in the library, use Recognition, which identifies materials directly from an image.

What it affects elsewhere — results from the Agent Hub are the same library entries — you can act on them (moodboard, quote, compare) exactly as you would from a Knowledge Base search.

7. Why it's public

What it is — the Knowledge Base is open at /knowledge-base with no sign-in required.

Why it matters — being public means it doubles as a shareable, customer-facing catalogue — you can send a customer the link or open it yourself without authenticating — while remaining your internal reference library at the same time.

What it affects elsewhere — the public surface only exposes published material entries; private, internal and agent-only documents never appear here (the Dashboard's Latest Knowledge feed follows the same rule), so what's public is safe to share.

8. Knowledge articles — shareable, SEO-friendly pages

What it is — beyond material entries, the Knowledge Base also publishes knowledge articles (guides, explainers, care/spec write-ups). Each published article now has its own real, slug-based web address at /knowledge-base/:slug Public — a proper page you can link to directly, not a pop-up.

Why it matters — these article pages are built to be found and shared: each carries proper search-engine metadata and structured data (JSON-LD) and is pre-rendered, so it can rank in Google and shows a rich preview (title, summary, image) when you paste the link into a chat, email or social post. It turns your knowledge library into a marketing surface, not just an internal reference.

How to use it

  1. Browse or search the Knowledge Base and open an article — it loads at its own /knowledge-base/:slug URL.
  2. Copy the address bar to share that exact article with a customer or on social — the link previews nicely and needs no sign-in.
  3. Articles are authored/managed by your team in the admin Knowledge Base area; only published ones appear publicly (the same public/private rule as material entries).

Good to know: the Knowledge Base reflects the catalogues that have been processed for your workspace. If a material seems to be missing, it usually means its source catalogue hasn't been added or finished processing yet — not that the platform can't represent it.
