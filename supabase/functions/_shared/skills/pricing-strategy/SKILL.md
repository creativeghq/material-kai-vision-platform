# Pricing Strategy

## When to use
Use this when discounting has become routine, win rates are healthy but margins are eroding, or the organization genuinely doesn't know whether its prices are too high, too low, or simply structured wrong for how customers actually derive value. It's also the right tool ahead of a new product or tier launch, when price is often set by copying a competitor or by gut feel rather than by any estimate of the value being created. The signal to use it now: sales reps have unofficial, well-known discount levels that exceed the official discount policy, which means the list price has stopped functioning as a real anchor. Without a rigorous pricing diagnosis, companies either leave value on the table by underpricing relative to what customers would actually pay, or bleed margin through undisciplined discounting that a value-based structure would have prevented.

## What it does
This skill separates a pricing problem into its actual root cause — a value-communication gap, a structural mismatch between price metric and value delivered, or a discounting-discipline failure — using willingness-to-pay evidence rather than opinion, and models the price-volume trade-off to find the margin-maximizing zone. It converts the diagnosis into a concrete, sequenced action plan covering structure, price fences, and discount governance, not just a new number.

## Method
1. Anchor to value first: estimate the economic value the offer creates for each customer segment (cost saved, revenue generated, risk reduced) relative to the next-best alternative, including doing nothing. This value estimate — not cost-plus or competitor matching — is the ceiling that should inform the pricing conversation.
2. Map current price realization against that value estimate by segment: where realized price (list price minus average discount) sits far below estimated value, that's a value-capture gap; where it sits above, that's a retention and churn risk waiting to surface.
3. Probe willingness to pay directly where data allows, using a structured price-sensitivity survey approach that identifies the range between "too cheap to be credible" and "too expensive to consider," triangulated against actual win/loss data at different discount levels rather than survey responses alone — stated willingness to pay is directionally useful but should never be taken as precise without behavioral confirmation.
4. Model price-volume sensitivity: estimate how a given price change would affect close rate and volume, and calculate margin at several price points to find the margin-maximizing zone rather than assuming the current price or the highest tested price is optimal.
5. Diagnose the structural fit of the pricing metric itself: does the company charge for the unit that actually tracks with the value customers receive (per seat, per transaction, per outcome), or has the metric drifted out of alignment as the product or customer base evolved? A structurally misaligned metric will keep generating pricing friction no matter how the number itself is adjusted.
6. Audit discounting behavior: pull the distribution of actual realized discounts by deal size, segment, and rep, and identify where the "unofficial" discount floor has drifted from the official policy — this frequently reveals the real problem is discipline, not the price itself.
7. Design price fences where willingness to pay clearly differs by segment (self-serve vs. enterprise, month-to-month vs. annual) so that value-based price differentiation is structurally enforced rather than left to ad hoc negotiation.
8. Translate the diagnosis into a sequenced action plan: which segment gets repriced first, what new structure or fences are introduced, what the new discount floor and approval process are, and how the change will be communicated to the sales team and existing customers to manage the transition.

## Inputs
- Current price list, actual realized prices, and discount data by segment, deal size, and rep
- Cost-to-serve by segment or product line
- Win/loss data, ideally with the discount level at which each deal closed or was lost
- Any existing willingness-to-pay research, or willingness to run a lightweight pricing survey

## Output format
A value-versus-realized-price map by segment; a willingness-to-pay range with behavioral triangulation against win/loss data; a price-volume sensitivity curve identifying the margin-maximizing zone; a discount-distribution audit flagging where actual practice has drifted from policy; and a sequenced pricing action plan covering structure changes, price fences, and a new discount governance process.

## Example
A B2B software company sees healthy win rates but declining margin and discovers average discretionary discounts have crept from an official 10% ceiling to a realized average of 27% in the enterprise segment. The value analysis shows enterprise customers are receiving roughly 4x the offer's price in quantifiable value, meaning the problem is not value perception but discount discipline. The action plan introduces a tiered value-based structure with a hard discount floor at 15%, requiring VP approval below that line, sequenced to launch with the next renewal cycle and communicated to the sales team alongside updated value-selling materials.

## Common pitfalls
- Treating a discounting problem as a pricing-level problem and cutting list price, which does nothing to fix an undisciplined approval process and simply resets a lower floor for the same behavior to recur at.
- Relying only on stated willingness-to-pay survey data without triangulating it against actual behavioral win/loss evidence at different discount levels.
- Setting one price for an entire customer base when willingness to pay clearly diverges by segment, leaving value uncaptured in some segments and creating churn risk in others.
