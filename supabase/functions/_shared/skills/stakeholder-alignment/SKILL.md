# Stakeholder Alignment

## When to use
Use this before any recommendation goes into a formal decision meeting where multiple stakeholders with different interests, incentives, and levels of influence will weigh in — and especially when past proposals of a similar kind have died in exactly that kind of meeting despite strong analytical merit. It's also the right tool when a decision is politically sensitive (a reorg, a resource reallocation away from an established team, a change that creates visible winners and losers) and the natural instinct is to walk into the room and let the analysis speak for itself. The signal to use it now: someone says "the logic is solid, we should be fine" about a decision that visibly threatens at least one powerful stakeholder's territory. Without deliberate stakeholder alignment, technically sound recommendations die in the room because objections that could have been anticipated and defused in advance instead surface live, unprepared for, in front of the full group.

## What it does
This skill identifies every stakeholder whose explicit or tacit support the decision requires, maps them by influence and interest to prioritize where effort should go, and builds a specific, sequenced engagement plan to move swing votes into support before the decision is ever formally put to the room. It closes by rehearsing how the actual decision meeting could go wrong and designs the pre-wiring needed to defuse that risk in advance.

## Method
1. List every stakeholder who has either formal decision authority over this recommendation or informal influence over those who do — don't limit the list to the org chart's obvious approvers; include influential voices a formal approver is known to listen to.
2. Map each stakeholder on a 2x2 of influence (their power to help or block the decision) versus interest (how much this decision affects them) — this determines where engagement effort should concentrate: high-influence, high-interest stakeholders need direct, early engagement; high-influence, low-interest stakeholders need brief, well-timed awareness rather than a full pitch.
3. Classify each key stakeholder's current position on the decision as a firm supporter, a firm opponent, or a genuine swing vote — and concentrate the engagement plan on swing votes, since firm supporters need reinforcement, not persuasion, and firm opponents are rarely converted by more of the same argument that already failed to move them.
4. Write a short motivation analysis for each key stakeholder: what outcome are they actually trying to protect or achieve (which may differ from their stated objection), and what specifically do they fear this decision will cost them (budget, headcount, credibility, control). Objections voiced in a meeting are often a proxy for this underlying motivation, not the real issue.
5. Design a specific engagement card for each swing-vote stakeholder: the tailored message that speaks to their actual motivation (not a generic version of the pitch), the right channel (one-on-one conversation versus a group briefing), and the right timing relative to the formal decision meeting.
6. Sequence the engagement plan deliberately: engage stakeholders whose support will make it easier to bring others along first, and save any stakeholder likely to be reassured by seeing others already on board for later in the sequence.
7. Run a pre-mortem on the formal decision meeting itself: imagine the meeting has just gone badly, and work backward to identify which stakeholder raised the objection that derailed it and why it wasn't defused beforehand.
8. Use that pre-mortem to finalize the pre-wire plan, explicitly closing the gap the pre-mortem revealed — if the pre-mortem shows a specific stakeholder's objection would sink the meeting, that stakeholder's one-on-one conversation happens before the room convenes, not during it.

## Inputs
- The recommendation and the decision it requires stakeholders to approve or support
- A list of stakeholders with formal or informal influence over the decision
- Any known history of this stakeholder group's past reactions to similar decisions
- The date and format of the formal decision meeting

## Output format
An influence-vs-interest stakeholder map with each person's current position (support/oppose/swing) marked; a motivation analysis for each key stakeholder; an engagement card per swing vote specifying message, channel, and timing; a sequenced pre-wire plan; and a pre-mortem of the formal decision meeting identifying the most likely failure point and how it's being defused in advance.

## Example
A contested reorganization proposal identifies two swing-vote stakeholders: a regional VP who fears losing direct reports, and a finance lead whose real concern (unstated in prior meetings) is that the new structure will complicate cost allocation reporting. The engagement plan sequences a one-on-one with the finance lead first, addressing the reporting concern directly with a proposed reporting workaround, then a one-on-one with the regional VP once the finance lead is on board, framing the change around a expanded strategic mandate rather than headcount loss. The pre-mortem flags that without this sequence, the regional VP would likely raise the objection publicly in the decision meeting and pull other undecided attendees along — exactly the scenario the pre-wire is designed to prevent.

## Common pitfalls
- Treating a stated objection in the meeting as the real issue, rather than digging into the underlying motivation or fear driving it beforehand.
- Spending equal engagement effort across all stakeholders instead of concentrating on the genuine swing votes, wasting effort reinforcing supporters and fruitlessly trying to convert firm opponents.
- Walking into the decision meeting without having run a pre-mortem, so the first time an objection surfaces is live, in front of the full group, with no prepared response.
